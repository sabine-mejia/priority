The psychology behind Priority

Why the Eisenhower Matrix actually works

Most productivity failures aren't about effort — they're about attention directed at the wrong things. The matrix gives your brain a framework to stop reacting and start deciding.

"What is important is seldom urgent, and what is urgent is seldom important."
— Dwight D. Eisenhower, 34th President of the United States
🧠

Cognitive load theory

Our working memory can hold only 4–7 items at a time. When every task feels equally pressing, the brain spends more energy managing the list than actually doing the work. Categorizing by urgency and importance reduces cognitive load by collapsing decisions into a simple two-axis framework.

Miller, G.A. (1956). "The magical number seven." Psychological Review.
⏳

Decision fatigue

Every decision depletes a finite pool of mental energy. Studies on judges, doctors, and executives show that decision quality degrades over the course of a day. By pre-sorting tasks before you start working, the matrix front-loads your decisions when mental energy is highest and eliminates mid-day triage.

Baumeister et al. (1998). "Ego depletion." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.
🔥

The urgency trap

Humans are wired to respond to urgency cues — flashing lights, pinging notifications, people waiting. This "mere urgency effect" causes us to prioritize time-sensitive tasks over more important but quieter ones, even when the payoff from the important task is objectively higher.

Zhu et al. (2018). "Mere urgency effect." Journal of Consumer Research.
🎯

Intentional attention

Neuroscience research on the prefrontal cortex shows that deliberate planning activates higher-order executive functions, overriding reactive emotional responses. Writing tasks down and assigning them a quadrant literally shifts processing from the amygdala (reactive) to the prefrontal cortex (strategic).

Arnsten, A.F.T. (2009). "Stress signalling pathways." Nature Reviews Neuroscience.
📋

The Zeigarnik effect

Unfinished tasks occupy mental bandwidth even when you're not working on them — your brain keeps an open loop for each one. Capturing tasks in a trusted system (like a master list) closes these loops, freeing up cognitive resources and reducing background anxiety.

Zeigarnik, B. (1927). "Über das Behalten von erledigten und unerledigten Handlungen." Psychologische Forschung.
🌱

Quadrant II is the growth zone

Research on high performers consistently shows they spend more time in "important but not urgent" activities — deep work, relationship-building, learning, planning. These tasks have the highest long-term payoff but are the first to get crowded out by reactive urgency. Protecting this quadrant is the core habit of effectiveness.

Covey, S.R. (1989). The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People.

What each quadrant is really for

Quadrant I
Do now

Crises and genuine deadlines. These deserve your full attention — but if Q1 is always overflowing, it's a signal that Q2 work is being neglected. Aim to shrink this quadrant over time.

Quadrant II
Schedule

The most valuable quadrant. Prevention, strategy, relationships, skill-building. Investing here is what keeps Q1 from exploding. This is where growth happens.

Quadrant III
Delegate

Feels urgent but doesn't serve your actual goals — often other people's priorities dressed as yours. Interruptions, many meetings, certain emails. Hand these off where possible.

Quadrant IV
Eliminate

Time sinks with no real payoff. Not truly urgent, not truly important. Mindless scrolling, busywork, trivial tasks. Be honest with yourself — and ruthless with this quadrant.

About Priority

Your calm corner for
getting things done.

Priority is a free, browser-based task manager built around the Eisenhower Matrix — a simple framework for deciding what actually deserves your attention.

🔒 Your data stays in your browser. Nothing is sent to any server. No accounts, no tracking, no ads — ever.

How to use Priority

1
Capture everything first

Use the All todos list at the bottom of the matrix to brain-dump tasks as they come to you. Don't worry about priority yet — just get them out of your head.

2
Prioritize when you're ready

Drag tasks from your list into one of the four quadrants. Ask yourself: is this urgent? Is it important? Be honest — most things aren't both.

3
Add tasks directly to a quadrant

Click anywhere on a colored tile to add a task directly to that quadrant. Hover over the tile to see the expand ↗ link, which opens a focused view of that quadrant.

4
Respect the "Do now" limit

You can only add 3 tasks to Do now (urgent + important). This is intentional — research shows that when everything is top priority, nothing is. If you hit the limit, take a moment to reprioritize.

5
Complete tasks

Click the circle on any task to mark it done. You'll be prompted to log how long it took and add any notes before it moves to your Completed archive.

6
Use the focus timer

Hit the ⏱ timer in the top-right to start a focus session. It defaults to 30 minutes but you can set any duration. Completed sessions are logged so you can track your focus hours over time.

A note on your data

prioritea stores everything locally in your browser using localStorage. This means your tasks persist between visits on the same browser — but they won't sync across devices, and clearing your browser cache will erase them. No cloud, no logins, no data collection of any kind.

Think of it like a sticky note that lives in your browser.

Too many urgent priorities

Cognitive research shows that labeling more than 3 tasks as both urgent and important leads to decision fatigue — your brain can't effectively prioritize them all, so nothing gets the attention it deserves.

Take a moment: is each task truly urgent and important? Try moving some to Schedule (important but not urgent) or Delegate.
